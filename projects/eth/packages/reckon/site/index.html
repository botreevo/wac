Attested derivations · wac-eth/packages/reckon

Know what you are about to run, and exactly what that rests on.

reckon names every result by its hash (an IPFS CID), re-runs how it was produced on a sealed WebAssembly machine on two engines, collects signed statements about it, and admits it only under a policy you write. The few facts that must be current, such as names and revocations, are read from Ethereum by storage proof, anchored by a light client from a checkpoint you choose.

How it works

  1. A recipe recipe → CID

    A program, its arguments and its input, put together as one directory tree. The recipe names that tree, the machine and the object format (unixfs-v1-2025+hidden), so a recipe's CID names one exact computation. The compiler is just another input.

  2. A sealed machine, on two engines wasm-gc/wac-host/2

    The program runs in an in-memory world holding only its input: no clock, no random bytes, no network, no environment. NaNs are made canonical by rewriting the module, so V8 and wasmtime give the same output tree (status, log, out/) bit for bit. A trap or a failing exit is an output too. A run past a runner's own limits makes no claim at all.

  3. Signed claims DAG-CBOR · EIP-712

    Each runner signs a derivation: recipe R gives output O. Reviewers sign endorsements and rejections; anyone can revoke their own. The claims form a graph (origins: what derived this? dependents: what came from it?) and travel through mirrors, which can withhold a claim but never forge one.

  4. Your policy decides claim admit

    A JSON5 file you own: whose keys count, which roots you accept, and per role what it takes: say, a derivation witnessed by two runners on two engines from inputs a reviewer endorsed. Admission walks the graph back to your roots, and says why it refuses.

  5. Anchored to Ethereum light client · storage proofs

    From a beacon checkpoint, the light client follows the sync committee (Altair through Fulu) to a finalized block. ENS names, on-chain revocations and rejection lists are read by proof against that block, so a lying node is refused rather than believed.

All of it is one command, from a checkpoint to a decision:

claim admit ens:vitalik.eth app --policy policy.json5 \
    --checkpoint 0x… --beacon-url https://… --rpc https://… --mirror https://…

and claim run does the same, then fetches the admitted bytes (every block checked against its CID) and runs them on the same sealed machine.

The capstone

Source to compiler to app to its run, each signed by two runners. The compiler is admitted only when the seed and wac's hand-written bootstrap ladder both build it (diverse double-compiling). A fresh client with an empty store resolves the app's name by proof, admits and runs it; a tampered output, a missing endorsement, a lying mirror and a revoked runner are each refused.

vitalik.eth, from a checkpoint

Recorded mainnet: the light client proves the block from a checkpoint, the name is read by proof, the policy decides. A different checkpoint, a node that lies about a header or a proof, and lying beacon answers are refused.

@N:admit-by-name@

The compiler builds itself

wac's compiler, inside the sealed machine, compiles its own 4,050-file source on V8 and on wasmtime, and that compiler compiles it again: the same bytes. A fixed point, witnessed by both engines.

@N:selfbuild@

The ladder meets the seed

wac's hand-written bootstrap ladder builds the compiler with no binary to start from; inside the machine it compiles wac to the same output, one CID, as the seed does. The first step to rooting trust at reviewed sources.

@N:ladder@

Assembling the bootstrap's root

The ladder starts from wasm written as text by hand. Assembling it is now a machine whose two runners are wac's Rust and JavaScript assemblers; they agree on every text, and the L1 interpreter is admitted only through both.

@N:l0@

Flattening wacc

The one step outside every rung: a module graph made one program for the L5 compiler. wac's two flatteners, as two runners, agree on wacc's 38 modules, byte for byte with the ladder.

@N:flatten@

Runners that earn their word

A runner shows it implements the machine by witnessing a suite of the machine's own cases. A policy counts its word only then: one that leaks NaNs, has no floor, or never ran the suite is left out.

@N:conformance@

Finding mirrors

One URL to every mirror: static manifests, served with CORS, naming mirrors and more manifests. What is found is only where else to look; a lying mirror's forged statement is left out and named.

@N:discovery@

Two runners, one policy

The smallest whole loop: one recipe on two engines, both runners sign, a reviewer endorses the inputs, the output is admitted, and taken away again by each kind of revocation.

@N:demo@

Claims through mirrors

Two signers publish into a mirror served by a plain web server; a client with an empty store admits from it. A tampered statement on the server is left out and named.

@N:claims-mirror@

Said plainly, because the point is to know exactly what an answer rests on.

The bootstrap's host toolsThe L0 assembler and the flattener run outside any wasm machine. Each is written twice by wac, in Rust and in JavaScript, and a policy asks both to sign the same answer: one bad tool, or one lying runner, is not enough.two of each
The engines and runnersV8 and wasmtime running the machine. Requiring two runners on two engines means one engine's bug, or one runner's lie, is not enough, and a policy can count a runner only once it has witnessed the machine's conformance suite.two of them
The cryptographySHA-256 for names, secp256k1 for signatures, keccak and the sync committee's BLS for Ethereum's proofs.standard

The devnet halves of some demos anchor to a block hash taken from the node, since a devnet has no beacon chain; the mainnet halves use the light client. A public IPFS gateway can serve content as one more mirror, trusted only to be there, with every block re-hashed; that path has not yet been seen to pass, because the gateway was down when it was tried. Installing it

  1. Unzip reckon-extension.zip into a folder.
  2. In Chrome, open chrome://extensions and turn on Developer mode.
  3. Load unpacked, and choose that folder. Click reckon's icon to open its page.
  4. In settings: a checkpoint (a beacon block root you trust), a beacon node and an execution RPC (both must allow cross-origin requests; the RPC must answer eth_getProof), your mirrors, and your policy (JSON5, as claim admit reads it). Save, then open a subject.

What it does not verify yet

  • Its JavaScript is reviewed, not derived: the extension's own pages, and the browser glue wac's build bundles with deno, are endorsed by the release's reviewer. The rest is derived.
  • Who built it: the runners' and the reviewer's keys were made for this release by one builder, so two engines agree, but two independent parties have not. A policy of your own names signers you trust.
  • The compiler: the policy's root is the wac compiler built natively at the pinned commit; deriving it from wac's hand-written bootstrap is decision D, under way.
  • Every app: static sites, apps of ES modules (imports, import(), module workers) and wac-web pages work; wac's --target browser apps do not (they need SharedArrayBuffer, which the sandbox cannot have), nor a worker's own fetch, nor URLs built from strings outside the tree.
  • Distribution: it is a zip to load unpacked, not a signed store listing.
milestones 4 and 5 of the plan

The spike: can an admitted app run with nothing else?

Five ways of showing an app were tried in a minimal Manifest V3 extension. Frames served by the extension's service worker either never load or get the extension's own origin and APIs. The one that works is a packaged sandbox page, handed the app inlined from verified bytes. It runs with origin null and no extension API, and it can fetch only within its own tree. A listener saw nothing reach the network: no XHR, remote image, beacon, WebSocket, frame or popup.
